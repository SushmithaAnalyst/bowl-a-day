International Students in the US by Origin Country

International enrollment reached a record 1.18 million students in 2024/25, the highest figure in IIE Open Doors history. India, China, and South Korea account for more than half of the total enrollment. These three groups, together with Nigerian and Vietnamese students, face the highest nutritional risk because their home-country dietary staples are the least available in standard US grocery chains.
Source: IIE Open Doors 2024/25 Report. India 363,019 and China 265,919 are exact figures from the press release. South Korea, Vietnam, Nigeria, Canada, and Mexico figures are estimated from 2023-24 baselines using documented growth rates (labeled est.).

Why Nutrition Becomes a Problem After Arrival

International students face a convergence of challenges that domestic students do not. Familiar foods are unavailable. Grocery environments are unfamiliar. Cultural eating patterns break down. Budget pressures force trade-offs. These challenges compound in the first one to two years before students find their footing.

Why Bowl A Day Addresses This Gap

Existing Approaches Fall Short

Nutrition pamphlets provide information students already have access to online. Dietary counseling is resource-intensive and low-reach. Neither approach addresses the practical skill of making food decisions under real-world constraints.

What a Game Can Do Differently

A game can simulate the actual experience of choosing food with limited money and limited access, watching the consequences unfold, and discovering what works. That kind of practice produces knowledge that stays.

Bowl A Day

A VR educational game where players explore an apartment environment, collect foods, build bowls, and experience the health consequences of their choices. Every food item, every deficiency effect, and every recovery outcome is grounded in verified nutrition science.

Why Nutritional Challenges Occur After Migration
When international students arrive in the US, the foods that made up their daily diet at home are often impossible to find outside specialty markets. This is not a matter of preference. These foods provided specific nutrients that are now missing from the diet. The gap opens quietly and widens over months.

What Happens When Nutritional Needs Are Not Met
Six nutrients are consistently at risk in international student populations. Each has documented prevalence data from NHANES population surveys and peer-reviewed clinical studies. Each produces measurable health consequences. Each connects directly to a mechanic in Bowl A Day.

Deficiency Prevalence by Nutrient and Population Group

All figures are directly measured from published studies: Iron — Gupta et al. PMC10300696; Vit D — Ginde et al. PMC6075634; B12 — Kapil et al. PMC6540890; Calcium — Bailey et al. PMC5748746; Folate — Bailey et al. PMC6567168; Zinc — Reider et al. PMC7352522 (US adults overall; vegetarians face elevated risk due to phytate absorption reduction).

Six Risk Profiles

Iron39.9% of females aged 12-21

Fatigue, reduced concentration, cold intolerance. Primary risk group: South Asian women and vegetarians where meat-based iron intake is low.

Vitamin D17.5% Non-Hispanic Black, 7.6% Asian

Low energy, bone density loss, immune suppression. Worsens in winter months and low-sun climates, which is when many students arrive.

Vitamin B1247% Indian vegetarian adults

Cognitive fog, difficulty concentrating, nerve health effects. B12 is found almost exclusively in animal products, making it the highest-risk nutrient for vegetarian students from India.

Calcium23% of Black women below EAR

Bone density loss, muscle cramps. Risk is elevated in students avoiding dairy, which is common in South Asian, East Asian, and West African populations due to lactose intolerance.

Omega-3Indian adults: highest AA:EPA ratio globally

Inflammation, mood effects, cognitive health implications. Indian vegetarian students show the greatest omega-3 imbalance because their diets are low in fish and high in omega-6 oils.

Zinc~15% of US adults inadequate (Reider et al.); elevated risk in plant-heavy diets

Immune weakness, slow wound healing. Zinc absorption is reduced by phytates in grains and legumes, which form the dietary foundation for many South and East Asian students. Vegetarians face elevated risk due to phytate-mediated absorption reduction documented in nutritional biochemistry literature.

Why Experiential Learning Produces Lasting Change

Concrete Experience

The player makes food decisions under real constraints -- budget limits, seasonal gaps, unfamiliar options. The experience is not simulated difficulty. It reflects real conditions.

Reflective Observation

The WII shows what changed and why. The player sees the link between the bowl they built and the state of the world around them.

Abstract Conceptualization

The player understands which nutrients were missing, which foods address each gap, and why certain combinations work better than others.

Active Experimentation

The player adjusts the bowl, tries different combinations, and discovers recovery foods. This is practice at the skill, not presentation of the information.

The World Instability Index -- Feedback Made Visible

The WII is a gameplay feedback mechanism. It translates nutritional quality into environmental experience. When the bowl is nutrient-poor, the world around the player destabilizes. When recovery foods are added, the world calms. The WII makes the normally invisible consequences of nutrition tangible.

The WII is a game mechanic. It is not a medical assessment tool.

How Bowl A Day Works
The player moves through a house or apartment environment. Food items are distributed throughout -- in the kitchen, refrigerator, pantry, and living areas. The player picks up foods, builds a bowl, and watches the nutritional consequences play out through health changes and the WII. Recovery foods bring the world back to stability. The player learns what worked and why.

How Learning Builds Across Sessions

Session 1 -- Discovery

Player encounters deficiency consequences for the first time without being told what to expect. Learns through surprise. Discovers that specific foods fix specific effects.

Session 2 -- Strategy

Player deliberately constructs a bowl based on what was learned. Tests different combinations. Explores cultural foods as substitutes for what they have at home.

Session 3 and Beyond -- Fluency

Player can explain which foods address which nutrients and why. Can adapt a bowl under seasonal and access constraints. Food-nutrient mapping becomes habitual.

What Makes the Learning Stick

Consequence Before Explanation

The player experiences the effect of iron deficiency before being told what caused it. Discovery-based learning produces stronger retention than direct instruction because the learner is already asking the question before the answer arrives.

Cultural Validation

The game includes traditional foods -- Ragi, Moringa, Fenugreek, Ugu -- and shows their nutritional value is high. Students discover that their food heritage is a nutritional asset, not a barrier to eating well in the US.

No Prerequisites Required

A student who has never heard of Vitamin D or iron can still build accurate understanding by playing. The game builds knowledge from the ground up through experience rather than relying on prior science education.

Why Bowl A Day Matters
Bowl A Day does not promise to solve food insecurity or replace dietary counseling. It builds specific capacities that students carry with them into every grocery trip, every meal decision, and every season of their time in the US.

Five Capacities Bowl A Day Builds

Nutrient Awareness

Students leave knowing which nutrients their background and lifestyle put them at greatest risk for -- and what the consequences look and feel like.

Food-Nutrient Relationships

Students build a mental map: fish provides Omega-3 and Vitamin D; eggs provide B12; spinach provides iron and folate. This map transfers directly to food selection decisions.

Healthy Eating Habits

Repeated bowl-building practice creates a habitual framework for thinking about meals in terms of nutritional balance rather than just taste or convenience.

Cultural Food Adaptation

Students discover that traditional foods are nutritionally valuable and that US equivalents are available. Cultural identity becomes a nutritional resource rather than a source of anxiety.

Nutritional Balance Over Time

The game teaches that good nutrition is not a single meal -- it is consistent, varied intake across seasons, budgets, and food environments. This is the central message the WII reinforces across sessions.

What Bowl A Day Does and Does Not Claim

What the Game Does

Builds understanding of how food choices affect nutrient intake and wellbeing. Teaches students to recognize their own nutritional situation and make better-informed adjustments. Creates awareness that generalizes across food environments, budgets, and seasons.

What the Game Does Not Do

Does not prescribe specific meals or meal plans. Does not provide medical diagnoses or clinical deficiency assessments. Does not replace professional dietary counseling or campus health services.

Why This Distinction Matters

Awareness and decision-making skills are durable. They generalize across changing budgets, new cities, new seasons, and new food environments. Specific recipes and prescriptions become outdated as circumstances change. Skills do not.
